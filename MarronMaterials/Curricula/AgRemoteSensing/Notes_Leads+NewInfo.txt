Total belowground biomass cannot be measured directly by NDVI, because satellite and drone sensors cannot see beneath the soil surface. Instead, scientists use NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) as an indirect proxy to estimate belowground biomass by relying on established relationships between aboveground plant vigor and belowground root mass.

How NDVI Connects to Belowground Biomass
	1. NDVI measures aboveground greenness: NDVI uses red and near-infrared light reflected by leaves to measure canopy density and photosynthetic health.
	2. Aboveground biomass correlates with NDVI: Higher NDVI values generally indicate denser, healthier aboveground vegetation (leaves and stems).
	3. Root-to-shoot ratios bridge the gap: Ecologists use region- or species-specific root-to-shoot ratios (or allometric equations) to convert NDVI-derived aboveground biomass into an estimate of belowground plant biomass.

Key Limitations and Caveats
	• It mainly estimates plant roots, not total soil life: Because NDVI reflects plant photosynthesis, its correlation is strongest with belowground phytomass (roots) rather than microbes, fungi, or soil fauna.
	• Environmental stress shifts root allocation: Plants in dry, nutrient-poor, or heavily grazed areas often invest heavily in deep roots while maintaining sparse leaves—meaning low NDVI does not always mean low belowground biomass.
	• Canopy saturation: In dense forests or thick grasslands, NDVI "saturates" (maxes out near 0.8–0.9) even as trees continue adding massive woody roots underground.
	• Soil type and climate matter: Accurate models usually combine NDVI with rainfall, soil moisture, temperature, and land-cover data rather than relying on NDVI alone.
