## 1. What Ashby's excerpt actually says

**The core result (§11/5–11/7).** Picture a game. The disturbance D picks a row of a table, and the regulator R picks a column. Assume no outcome repeats within a column. Then the variety (number of distinct outcomes) can never fall below D's variety divided by R's variety. Measured in bits, this becomes **V_E ≥ V_D − V_R**. Ashby's short version is "only variety can destroy variety."

**Extensions that matter for resilience.**
- **The world itself can absorb variety (§11/9).** The fixed world T may soak up some disturbance with no help from R. This gives V_E ≥ V_D − log k − V_R, where log k is the variety T absorbs on its own. In resilience terms, that is passive buffering, or designed-in robustness.
- **Information-theory form (§11/8).** In Shannon's entropy terms, H(E) ≥ H(D) + H_D(R) − H(R). The outcome is best when R is a fixed function of D. In other words, the regulator's response rule must already exist before the disturbance arrives.
- **Channel limit (§11/11).** "R's capacity as a regulator cannot exceed R's capacity as a channel of communication." This puts hard limits on both sensing (D→R) and acting (R→T).
- **A logical necessity, not a physical one (§11/10).** The law comes from how a table can be arranged, not from properties of matter. No invention escapes it.
- **Regulation enables control (§11/14).** A perfect regulator gives a higher-level controller C full control over the outcome. This is a layered structure.
- **Constancy of relations, not of states (§11/15).** A searchlight or a steering wheel moves constantly in order to hold one relationship fixed.
- **Redrawing the boundary (§11/17–21).** Noise, unknown starting states, and multiple threats can all be folded into a larger disturbance D. The cost is that R must become more complex.
- **Taking in variety on purpose (§11/13).** Organisms deliberately take in more variety than they currently need, through curiosity and play. This builds up the regulator's repertoire.

**Text glitches in the excerpt.** A few symbols appear garbled:
- "≥" is printed as ">" in §11/8–9.
- "106 bits" in Ex. 3 is 10⁶.
- "acceptable set 17" is η.

## 2. The law framed as resilience (Diagram 1)

Read through a resilience lens, the law gives a clear condition:

**The system is resilient when V_D − log k − V_R ≤ log|η|.**

Here η is the viability envelope, the set of acceptable values for the essential variables E. In words: whatever disturbance variety is not absorbed by the world (log k) or cancelled by the regulator (V_R) must still fit inside what the system can tolerate.

**Brittleness is a variety deficit.** Any variety left over flows through T to E and pushes the essential variables out of η.

The diagram maps this condition onto Ashby's D–T–R–E–C loop. It also lists five resilience levers the law implies:
1. Increase the regulator's repertoire (V_R).
2. Absorb more disturbance passively in T (log k).
3. Widen the sensing and acting channels.
4. Redraw the system boundary.
5. Take in variety before a crisis demands it.

A small chart shows a "resilience margin." The same regulator copes with routine disturbance but fails once a surprise raises V_D.

## 4. Synthesis

**Where the two disagree in method.**
- The law is a **necessary but not sufficient** condition for every Woods concept.
- Ashby gives a *deductive limit with a metric*, measured in bits.
- Woods gives an *empirical taxonomy*: patterns of failure plus a research agenda.

**The deepest contrast is what each takes as fixed.** Ashby assumes the disturbance set, the world T, and the target η are all given. Woods' progression from R1 to R4 can be read as successively removing those assumptions:
- surprise breaks the disturbance set;
- adaptation changes the world;
- co-evolution changes everything over time.

**Where they connect.** Ashby's own hints point toward Woods: redrawing boundaries, deliberately taking in surplus variety, and layered control. Those passages are the bridge from requisite variety to graceful extensibility and sustained adaptability.
