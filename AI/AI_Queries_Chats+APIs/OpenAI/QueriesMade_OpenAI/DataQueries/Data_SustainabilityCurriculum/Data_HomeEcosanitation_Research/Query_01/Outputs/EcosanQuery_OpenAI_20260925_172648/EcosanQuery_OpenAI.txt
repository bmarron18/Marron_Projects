The key design issue is separating fast decomposition from reliable sanitization. I’m developing a batch-based concept with separate urine storage, insulated feces composting, and a later fungal-curing stage, while checking which inoculation benefits have actually been demonstrated—and whether they transfer to a three-adult household.## Recommended concept

I recommend a **waterless, urine-diverting toilet connected to two separate treatment lines**:

- **Urine:** contained batch storage—or validated solar/waste-heat pasteurization—producing a liquid fertilizer.
- **Feces:** insulated aerobic co-composting, followed by a separate fungal-curing stage, producing a mature soil amendment.

This combines established ecosanitation principles with an **experimental, staged microbial-inoculation enhancement**. The crucial distinction is that **accelerated decomposition is not the same as reliable sanitization**: inoculants must not replace verified treatment conditions and product-quality checks. ([eawag.ch](https://www.eawag.ch/en/department/sandec/publications/compendium/?utm_source=openai))

The design below is a complete **conceptual treatment chain**, not a construction-ready or validated appliance. Its dimensions and resource budgets are preliminary engineering allowances.

**Downloads:** [Six-page design brief with references](sandbox:/mnt/data/ecosan_design_brief.pdf) · [Scalable process diagram](sandbox:/mnt/data/ecosan_concept.svg)

## 1. What the literature supports

The most useful findings from the reviewed material are:

- **Source separation is the sound starting point.** Eawag’s sanitation manuals describe separate collection, treatment, and reuse pathways rather than treating a toilet alone as a complete sanitation system. That approach informs the design here. ([eawag.ch](https://www.eawag.ch/en/department/sandec/publications/compendium/?utm_source=openai))
- **Microbial inoculation can help, but does not consistently shorten processing time.** A controlled cattle-manure study improved organic-matter degradation and compost maturity, yet found no significant reduction in total composting time. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7035406/?utm_source=openai))
- **Stage-specific inoculation is promising.** A study published on **August 11, 2026**, investigated different microbial consortia for successive cow-manure composting stages. It supports testing staged inoculation—not assuming that one “effective microorganisms” mixture will work throughout the process. ([frontiersin.org](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/microbiomes/articles/10.3389/frmbi.2026.1855343/full))
- **Fungi belong principally in the cooler, fiber-degrading stage.** An agricultural-waste drum experiment found that adding *Phanerochaete chrysosporium* after the thermophilic phase improved decomposition more than adding it initially. This is relevant to straw and woody cover material, but is not validation of a household human-feces treatment process. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25567055/))
- **Cold spots are a major sanitation weakness.** Research on human-excreta composting in Haiti identified cooler pile corners and emphasized redistribution and extended processing. A hot center alone is insufficient evidence of treatment. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5453478/))

**Design implication:** prioritize containment, batch identity, insulation, moisture, airflow, and temperature verification. Treat specialized inoculants as a measurable improvement to that foundation.

## 3. Preliminary sizing for three adults

Published excretion measurements vary substantially with diet and fluid intake. For preliminary sizing, I use **6 L/day of urine and 0.6 kg/day of wet feces for the household**, rather than sizing tightly around average values. Measure actual inputs before final design. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26246784/?utm_source=openai))

| Component | Proposed allowance |
|---|---|
| Toilet | One waterless urine-diverting fixture with dedicated ventilation |
| Urine capacity | Six 500-L tanks, each limited to approximately 450 L working volume |
| Primary solids vessels | Three insulated 700-L vessels, approximately 450 L working volume each |
| Curing vessels | Four 600-L vessels, approximately 500 L working volume each |
| Total co-compost feed | Initially budget 4–8 L/day, including feces, paper, cover material, and co-feed |
| Batch collection interval | Up to eight weeks; 8 L/day × 56 days = 448 L |
| Electricity | Preliminary target: 50–150 Wh/day, **excluding heating** |
| Service area | Approximately 12–18 m², excluding the toilet and nutrient-receiving garden |

The three primary vessels serve as **filling, active treatment, and spare/turning capacity**. The curing vessels provide approximately six months of residence capacity without depending on an assumed volume reduction.

The urine tanks are intentionally substantial: long treatment residence and seasonal restrictions on fertilizer application require storage. Faster validated urine treatment could reduce this footprint.

## 4. Written treatment design

### A. Collection and containment

Use a roofed, flood-protected service enclosure with an impermeable, curbed floor, spill capture, pest exclusion, and separate access for untreated material and finished products. Route urine by gravity through accessible plumbing; keep feces in covered, ventilated containment. These choices follow the complete-service-chain approach in sanitation guidance. ([eawag.ch](https://www.eawag.ch/en/department/sandec/publications/compendium/?utm_source=openai))

My proposed implementation would include:

- Individually isolated urine tanks with level indicators and batch labels.
- A direct feces chute where practical, or small reusable transfer containers.
- Dedicated tools for untreated and treated material.
- A contained sump for dirty rinse water and leachate.
- Outdoor exhaust, with an odor biofilter that is **not credited as a pathogen barrier**.

**No untreated liquid should drain into the garden.** Return contaminated liquids only to material that will subsequently undergo the complete treatment process.

### B. Urine treatment

**Lowest-electricity route: warm batch storage.** WHO’s larger-system guidance specifies at least six months at 20°C for its broadest crop-use category, with stated urine-mixture conditions and additional application precautions. The residence period begins **after the last addition**, not when tank filling starts. Heavy fecal contamination requires reassessment. Do not acidify urine while relying on this alkaline-storage treatment route. ([iris.who.int](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/78265/9241546824_eng.pdf?+isAllowed=y&sequence=1&utm_source=openai))

For this design:

- Six tanks provide **2,700 L usable capacity**.
- At 6 L/day, each tank takes **75 days** to fill.
- Label each tank with its closing date and verified treatment conditions.
- Do not continually top up a tank that is undergoing treatment.

**Cold-climate alternative:** install an engineered solar-thermal or waste-heat pasteurization module. Rich Earth Institute reports treating urine at **80°C for 90 seconds**; a household adaptation would need cold-point verification and protection against recontamination. ([richearthinstitute.org](https://richearthinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Urine-My-Garden-2026-Plain-Text-1.pdf))

The ideal heat requirement for 6 L/day heated from 20°C to 80°C is:

\[
Q=\frac{6\times4.18\times60}{3600}
\approx0.42\ \text{kWh thermal/day}
\]

That excludes losses. Consequently, **unheated winter storage cannot simply be assumed equivalent to warm storage**, and electric heating would materially change the energy budget.

### C. Fecal co-composting

Use local chopped straw, leaves, and untreated wood shavings for structure, together with suitable wet kitchen or garden residues. Do not assume three adults’ feces alone will reliably maintain sanitizing temperatures. Co-composting and insulated vessels are design responses to that small loading. ([eawag.ch](https://www.eawag.ch/en/department/sandec/publications/fsm-book/?utm_source=openai))

Proposed commissioning targets are:

- Approximately **50–60% moisture**.
- Initial **C:N around 25–30:1**, adjusted to actual feedstock.
- A porous mixture and controlled airflow.
- Insulated walls and lid.
- Temperature logging at the center, walls, base, and other identified cold locations.

These are operating targets, not a universal scoop-based recipe; sanitation guidance emphasizes suitable process conditions and whole-mass temperature monitoring. ([eawag.ch](https://www.eawag.ch/en/department/sandec/publications/fsm-book/?utm_source=openai))

Once a batch closes, **add no fresh feces**. Plan initially for **4–8 weeks of active treatment after closure**, but regard that as a prototype target—not a guaranteed completion time.

### D. Staged bacterial–fungal inoculation

**Early stage—bacterial decomposition.** Trial a professionally supplied, documented compost inoculant containing suitable cellulolytic or proteolytic strains, such as screened *Bacillus subtilis*. Compare its performance against a verified mature-compost starter without specialized inoculants. Species names alone do not establish strain safety or performance. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7035406/?utm_source=openai))

**Cooling stage—fungal fiber decomposition.** After successful heat treatment, trial a screened lignocellulose-degrading fungus. *Phanerochaete chrysosporium* is a relevant **research candidate**, not a proven consumer ecosanitation additive. Introduce it only below its strain-specific temperature limit; the main purpose is to process residual plant fibers. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25567055/))

I would **not specify a universal inoculation dose or promise a percentage acceleration** from the available evidence. Nor would I recommend home isolation of organisms from feces or copying an unscreened research consortium.

### E. Sanitization, curing, and release

For reference, U.S. Part 503 specifies these composting process benchmarks:

- **In-vessel/aerated static:** at least 55°C for three days.
- **Windrow:** at least 55°C for 15 days, including at least five turnings.

These are **not automatic approval or Class A certification for a homemade sanitation system**. ([ecfr.gov](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-O/part-503/appendix-Appendix%20B%20to%20Part%20503))

The proposed system must demonstrate treatment of the whole batch. Failed cold spots, missing records, or inadequate mixing mean **hold and reprocess**, not “use because it looks finished.” ([nepis.epa.gov](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=P1017HWO.TXT))

After successful treatment, allow approximately **six months of separate curing during initial operation**. This is a conservative design reserve, not an independent safety guarantee. Release should require:

1. Acceptable process records.
2. Stability and maturity assessment.
3. Accredited-laboratory pathogen testing appropriate to local requirements.
4. Approval for the intended land application.

Compost maturity and pathogen control are separate questions; human-excreta research supports retaining both treatment and curing stages. ([eawag.ch](https://www.eawag.ch/en/department/sandec/publications/fsm-book/?utm_source=openai))

## 5. Ecological footprint and practical limits

### Electricity and water

An illustrative electrical budget is:

- 2-W continuous ventilation: 48 Wh/day.
- 10-W blower for four hours: 40 Wh/day.
- 0.5-W logger/control system: 12 Wh/day.

**Total: 100 Wh/day, or 36.5 kWh/year**, before heating. Actual blower selection requires airflow and pressure testing.

The toilet requires **no flush water**. However, “virtually no freshwater” must not mean eliminating safe handwashing. Cleaning and moisture management also need explicit provision; contaminated wash water remains part of the treatment chain. ([eawag.ch](https://www.eawag.ch/en/department/sandec/publications/compendium/?utm_source=openai))

### Garden capacity may be the limiting factor

Apply urine according to soil tests and plant demand—not according to how quickly tanks fill. Incorporate it into soil rather than spraying foliage, and assess salinity risks, particularly in dry climates or greenhouses. ([richearthinstitute.org](https://richearthinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Urine-My-Garden-2026-Plain-Text-1.pdf))

For illustration only, **1,640 L/year at an assumed measured concentration of 6 g N/L contains 9.84 kg nitrogen**. At a hypothetical annual application allowance of 10 g N/m², that corresponds to about **984 m²** before accounting for other fertilizers and losses.

Thus, a small vegetable patch may not absorb the household’s entire nutrient output. A genuinely self-contained system needs sufficient planted area; otherwise it needs an authorized outlet.

### What should not be promised

This design should not be presented as:

- Producing verified garden-ready fecal compost in days.
- Removing all pharmaceuticals or persistent chemicals.
- Operating indefinitely without carbon inputs, maintenance, or testing.
- Achieving year-round sanitization in every climate without supplemental heat.

Advanced urine-recovery systems offer additional treatment possibilities, but introduce their own equipment, consumables, and operating requirements. ([richearthinstitute.org](https://richearthinstitute.org/research-results/pharmaceutical-study/?utm_source=openai))

**Bottom line:** this is a credible architecture to prototype, but its strongest features are separation, containment, controlled batches, and verification—not a “supermicrobe” mixture. Climate, co-feed availability, and nutrient-receiving land are the three site conditions to establish next.
